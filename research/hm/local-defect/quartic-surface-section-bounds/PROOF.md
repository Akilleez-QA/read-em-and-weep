# Quartic surface section bounds and exclusion of rank four

## Results

Over C, let Z be a dimension-two closed subscheme of P4 whose nonzero complete quartic system has no fixed divisor. Allow nonreduced structure, embedded components, and lower-dimensional components. Then:

1. If deg Z >= 10, then h0(I_Z(4)) <= 7.
2. If deg Z = 9, then h0(I_Z(3)) <= 2 and h0(I_Z(4)) <= 10.
3. In case 2, if h0(I_Z(3))=0, then h0(I_Z(4)) <= 8.

These statements require no arithmetic Cohen--Macaulay hypothesis. Combined with the established Heisenberg equivariance and rank-four section sequences, they exclude every remaining rank-four second-Chern pair (2,14), (7,9), (12,4), and hence exclude rank four.

## 1. A residual length lemma on P2

Let f,a,b,C be nonzero homogeneous forms, with deg f=t>0, deg a=deg b=s, deg C=4. Suppose a,b are coprime and f,C are coprime. Let W=V(fa,fb,C). Its support is finite: outside V(f), the first two equations cut out V(a,b), while on V(f) the equation C cuts a finite scheme. Then

    length W <= 4t+s^2.                                      (1)

To prove this with scheme multiplicities, put J=(fa,fb,C). The multiplication-by-f residual exact sequence, sheafified on P2, is

    0 -> (O/(J:f))(-t) -> O/J -> O/(f,C) -> 0.

The colon ideal contains a and b. Thus its sheaf quotient is a quotient of the complete-intersection structure sheaf O/V(a,b), of length s^2. The right term is the complete intersection of f and C, of length 4t. All three sheaves have finite support. Additivity of length proves (1). In particular the argument remains valid where the residual points lie on V(f), and with arbitrary nonreduced intersection multiplicities.

If a finite scheme D is contained in W, its length is at most this bound.

## 2. Plane quartic systems

Let D be a finite subscheme of P2 of length d, whose complete quartic system has no fixed curve. For any fixed nonzero form f, one can choose a quartic C in I_D(4) coprime to f: for each of the finitely many irreducible factors of f, divisibility defines a proper linear subspace of I_D(4), and finitely many proper linear subspaces do not cover this vector space over C.

### 2.1 No quadrics when d >= 9

If q is a nonzero quadric in I_D, choose C coprime to q as above. Then D is contained in the complete intersection V(q,C), of length eight. Therefore d>=9 implies I_D(2)=0, and also I_D(1)=0.

### 2.2 At most one cubic when d >= 10

Suppose q1,q2 are independent cubics in I_D. Their gcd has degree t in {0,1,2}. If t=0, their complete intersection has length nine and contains D. If t>0, write q1=fa, q2=fb, with a,b coprime of degree 3-t; choose a quartic C in I_D coprime to f. By (1),

    d <= 4t+(3-t)^2,

which equals eight for t=1 and nine for t=2. Thus in every case d<=9. Consequently d>=10 implies dim I_D(3)<=1.

### 2.3 At most two cubics when d=9

Suppose instead dim I_D(3)>=3. Let f be the gcd of every member of this cubic vector space, with degree t. Then t<=2, since a fixed cubic factor would make the space one-dimensional.

There exist two members whose gcd is exactly f. Here is an elementary justification. Divide the space by f to obtain a vector space U of forms with gcd one. Choose nonzero u in U. For every irreducible factor p of u, the subspace of U divisible by p is proper, since U has gcd one. Choose v outside their finite union. Then u and v are coprime.

If t=0, these two cubics cut out a complete intersection of length nine containing D. Since D also has length nine, its structure sheaf equals that of this complete intersection. The degree-three ideal of a (3,3) complete intersection is exactly the two-dimensional span of its defining cubics, a contradiction. This last assertion follows directly from its Koszul ideal resolution 0 -> O(-6) -> O(-3)^2 -> I_D -> 0 and H1(O(-3))=0 on P2.

If t=1, (1) applied to the two members and a quartic coprime to f gives d<=8, a contradiction.

If t=2, dividing the cubic space by f gives a subspace of H0(O_P2(1)) of dimension at least three. It is therefore the whole three-dimensional space. Its linear forms generate the unit ideal sheaf on P2, so the ideal sheaf generated by all the cubics is (f). Choosing a quartic C coprime to f shows that D is contained in V(f,C), of length eight, again a contradiction. Thus dim I_D(3)<=2 when d=9.

## 3. Hilbert functions and quartics on the plane

Write H_D(m)=binom(m+2,2)-h0(I_D(m)) for the Hilbert function of the saturated coordinate ring. A linear form avoiding the finite support is a nonzerodivisor on this ring. Its quotient is a standard graded Artinian algebra, so the first differences of H_D are nonnegative and, once zero, remain zero. Consequently H_D is nondecreasing, has eventual value d, and strictly increases at the next step whenever its present value is less than d.

For d>=10, Section 2 gives H_D(3)>=9. If H_D(3)=9, strict growth gives H_D(4)>=10; if H_D(3)>=10, monotonicity gives the same conclusion. Therefore

    h0(I_D(1))=h0(I_D(2))=0,
    h0(I_D(3))<=1, h0(I_D(4))<=5.               (2)

For d=9, Section 2 gives H_D(3)>=8. The same strict-growth or monotonicity dichotomy yields H_D(4)>=9. Therefore

    h0(I_D(1))=h0(I_D(2))=0,
    h0(I_D(3))<=2, h0(I_D(4))<=6.               (3)

The nonzerodivisor assertion also holds for nonreduced finite schemes: saturation eliminates an irrelevant associated prime, and all remaining associated primes are support-point primes, avoided by the chosen linear form. Equivalently, multiplication is injective on the homogeneous coordinate ring because the section is invertible on the projective scheme and the ideal is saturated.

## 4. Passing from surfaces to plane sections

Choose a general flag L=P2 inside H=P3 inside P4 and put C=Z intersect H and D=C intersect L scheme-theoretically. Choose the hyperplane equations successively to avoid every associated point of O_Z and O_C, so that both are nonzerodivisors on the corresponding structure sheaves. Then C has dimension one, and D is finite of length deg Z, by two differences of the Hilbert polynomial. Embedded and lower-dimensional components cause no difficulty: the proper hyperplanes avoid their associated points as required, and lower-degree polynomial terms vanish after the two differences.

The base scheme B of the complete quartic system of Z has dimension at most two, since the system has no fixed divisor. A general plane L meets B in a finite scheme or not at all. Choose the flag with this property as well. Hence the restricted global quartics have no fixed curve in L, and the larger complete quartic system of D likewise has no fixed curve.

There are exact ideal-sheaf restriction sequences

    0 -> I_Z(m-1) -> I_Z(m) -> I_(C,H)(m) -> 0,
    0 -> I_(C,H)(m-1) -> I_(C,H)(m) -> I_(D,L)(m) -> 0.

Their exactness uses only the two nonzerodivisor choices, which eliminate the Tor terms; there is no assertion of surjectivity on global sections. Denote the respective global-section dimensions by z_m,c_m,d_m. Since z_0=c_0=0, the sequences give

    z_m <= z_(m-1)+c_m,
    c_m <= c_(m-1)+d_m.

In all cases considered d_1=d_2=0. It follows that z_1=z_2=c_1=c_2=0, and

    c_3<=d_3, z_3<=d_3,
    c_4<=d_3+d_4,
    z_4<=2d_3+d_4.                              (4)

For degree>=10, (2) and (4) give z_4<=7. For degree nine, (3) and (4) give z_3<=2 and z_4<=10. If z_3=0, retain that exact value in the first recurrence to get

    z_4<=c_4<=d_3+d_4<=8.

This proves all three geometric statements.

## 5. Application: rank four is excluded

Use the established rank-four determinant branch k=4 and equivariant exact sequence

    0 -> F -> J tensor O -> R -> 0.

Here rank F=8, rank R=12, E=F* has rank eight, and both E and R have c1=4. The established strict positivity and full-support Heisenberg Chern congruences leave exactly

    (c2(E),c2(R)) = (2,14), (7,9), or (12,4).

Both sheaves have at least twenty global sections. The established general-section quotient argument supplies

    0 -> O^7  -> E -> I_(Z_E)(4) -> 0,
    0 -> O^11 -> R -> I_(Z_R)(4) -> 0,

where the schemes have dimensions two and degrees c2(E),c2(R), respectively. Their quartic systems have no fixed divisor: E is generated outside codimension two and R is globally generated, and their respective ideal-sheaf quotients inherit generation there. Since H1(O_P4)=0,

    h0(I_(Z_E)(4))>=13, h0(I_(Z_R)(4))>=9.

If the pair is (2,14), the degree>=10 bound gives h0(I_(Z_R)(4))<=7, a contradiction. If the pair is (12,4), it gives h0(I_(Z_E)(4))<=7, also a contradiction.

It remains to treat (7,9). Apply the degree-nine result to Z_R. Twisting its displayed quotient sequence by O(-1) and using H0(O(-1))=H1(O(-1))=0 yields a vector-space isomorphism

    H0(R(-1)) = H0(I_(Z_R)(3)).

The chosen sections and the quotient scheme need not be equivariant: this isomorphism is needed only for dimensions. The left vector space carries the natural Heisenberg representation because R is equivariant. The center has weight three on R, while O(-1) has weight one; thus the center acts on R(-1), and on its global sections, by the nontrivial scalar zeta^4. Every such finite-dimensional Heisenberg representation has dimension divisible by five (the standard cyclic-eigenspace argument).

The geometric bound gives 0<=h0(I_(Z_R)(3))<=2. Divisibility by five forces it to be zero. The sharpened degree-nine quartic bound now gives h0(I_(Z_R)(4))<=8, contradicting the lower bound nine. This excludes the last pair and hence rank four.
