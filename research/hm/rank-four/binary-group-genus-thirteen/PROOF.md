# Binary icosahedral genus obstruction through thirteen

## Results and scope

Let G=SL₂(F₅). There is no faithful G-action on a smooth connected complex projective curve of genus at most thirteen.

Consequently, in the Schrödinger normalizer setting A≅F₅² normal in N with N/A≅G acting faithfully and standardly on A:

1. There is no N-stable integral curve in P⁴ of degree at most 45.
2. Every nonzero N-invariant reduced curve with no line or conic components has degree at least 50.
3. Under the imported rank-four hypotheses, the reduced curve part Γ₄ is either empty or satisfies 50≤deg Γ₄≤125, with degree divisible by five.

The rank-four assertion imports the no-line theorem in ../rank-four-line-component-exclusion/PROOF.md, the no-conic theorem in ../rank-four-canonical-conic-exclusion/PROOF.md, and their dependencies, including dim Z₅≤1 and the twenty-dimensional coefficient-image result. It does not prove Γ₄ empty. No source tensor, source coefficients, actual source inputs, or problem-specific matrix is evaluated here.

## 1. Riemann–Hurwitz and the complete short signature list

The genus-at-most-six obstruction in ../normalizer-small-curve-exclusion/ADDENDUM_DEGREE_30.md, Section A, already handles genera zero and one. We give a uniform argument for genera 2≤h≤13.

The nonidentity element orders in G are exactly 2,3,4,5,6,10. For completeness, a determinant-one 2-by-2 matrix over F₅ has either distinct eigenvalues in F₅ (orders dividing four), distinct conjugate eigenvalues in F₂₅ of norm one (orders dividing six), or repeated eigenvalues ±1 (scalar, or a nontrivial unipotent times ±I, of order five or ten). This covers all cases. Its unique involution is z=−I, since an order-two matrix is diagonalizable in characteristic five and determinant one forces equal signs.

Let q be the quotient genus and e₁,…,eᵣ the nontrivial inertia orders. Stabilizers are cyclic because a finite stabilizer acts faithfully on the tangent line in characteristic zero. Riemann–Hurwitz gives

    (h−1)/60 = 2q−2 + Σ(1−1/eᵢ),       0 < (h−1)/60 ≤1/5.

If q≥2 the right side is at least two. If q=1 it is zero or at least one half. Hence q=0. Positivity forces r≥3, and each summand is at least one half, so r≥5 would give a right side at least one half. Thus r=3 or 4.

Here is the complete list of unordered signatures whose RH value is positive and at most 1/5; every listed value gives integral h:

    signature       h
    (2,3,10)        5
    (2,4,5)         4
    (2,4,6)         6
    (2,4,10)       10
    (2,5,5)         7
    (2,5,6)         9
    (2,5,10)       13
    (2,6,6)        11
    (3,3,4)         6
    (3,3,5)         9
    (3,3,6)        11
    (3,4,4)        11
    (2,2,2,3)      11

This enumeration does not depend on a search. For sorted triples (a,b,c), the condition is 4/5≤1/a+1/b+1/c<1. If a≥4 the sum is at most 3/4. For a=3, b=3 permits c=4,5,6; b=4 permits only c=4; and b≥5 is impossible. For a=2, b=2 gives sum greater than one; b=3 permits only c=10; b=4 permits c=5,6,10; b=5 permits c=5,6,10; b=6 permits only c=6; and b=10 is impossible. For four entries, the all-two tuple has zero RH value. The smallest positive change is replacing one two by three, giving 1/6; replacing it by four gives 1/4, and replacing two twos by threes gives 1/3. Thus (2,2,2,3) is the sole four-entry candidate.

## 2. Monodromy excludes twelve of the thirteen signatures

A connected Galois cover of the sphere has branch cycles x₁,…,xᵣ generating G, with exact orders eᵢ and product one. This follows by taking monodromy of the punctured sphere: its standard peripheral loops generate with their product as the sole relation.

In a three-entry signature containing a two, one branch cycle is z. The product relation expresses one of the other two as the inverse of z times the remaining one. Consequently the entire generated subgroup is generated by a central element and one other element; it is abelian, not G. In (2,2,2,3) the first three cycles all equal z, and the product relation would force the last one to equal z, contradicting its order three.

The remaining signatures other than (3,3,5) are (3,3,4), (3,3,6), (3,4,4). Pass to G/⟨z⟩≅A₅. Order-four elements project to involutions and order-six elements project to order-three elements. Therefore these signatures would generate A₅ by triples of orders (3,3,2), (3,3,3), or (3,2,2), respectively, with product one.

For the first two cases consider the two three-cycles in the usual five-letter action of A₅. If their supports meet in one letter, their product is a five-cycle (read the two cycles consecutively from their common letter). Otherwise the union of their supports has at most four letters, and they generate a subgroup fixing a letter. Thus a product of order two or three cannot generate A₅. For (3,2,2), the two involutions generate a dihedral group: if they are s,t, every word is (st)^k or (st)^k s, and st has order three by the product relation. Their generated subgroup has at most six elements. This completes all these exclusions without a triangle-group classification.

## 3. The (3,3,5) trace obstruction, with an elementary proof

Suppose x,y,w generate G with exact orders 3,3,5 and xyw=I. Work in the defining two-dimensional representation over F₅, and then over its algebraic closure. An order-three element has eigenvalues λ,λ⁻¹ with λ²+λ+1=0, so trace −1. An order-five element is nonidentity unipotent, hence trace 2, as is its inverse. Therefore

    tr x=tr y=−1,    tr(xy)=2.

For determinant-one matrices X,Y put a=tr X, b=tr Y, c=tr(XY). Cayley–Hamilton gives X⁻¹=aI−X, Y⁻¹=bI−Y. Expanding XYX⁻¹Y⁻¹ and taking traces, using tr(XY²)=bc−a, tr(XYX)=ac−b, and tr((XY)²)=c²−2, gives the identity

    tr(XYX⁻¹Y⁻¹) = a²+b²+c²−abc−2.

At (a,b,c)=(−1,−1,2), this equals 2, including in characteristic five.

Diagonalize X=x as diag(λ,λ⁻¹); these eigenvalues are distinct. Write Y=y as [[u,v],[w,t]], with ut−vw=1. Direct multiplication gives

    tr(XYX⁻¹Y⁻¹) = 2−(λ−λ⁻¹)² v w.

Thus vw=0. If w=0, both matrices preserve the first eigenline; if v=0 they preserve the second eigenline. They are simultaneously triangularizable over the algebraic closure. The invertible upper triangular group is solvable: the diagonal quotient is abelian and the strictly upper-unitriangular kernel is abelian. Every subgroup is therefore solvable. But x,y generate G because w=(xy)⁻¹, and G has nonsolvable quotient A₅. This contradiction eliminates the last signature and proves the genus obstruction.

## 4. Integral N-stable curves through degree 45

Use the same irreducible Schrödinger representation V as in ../rank-three-curve-constraints/PROOF.md. An A-stable integral curve C spans P⁴: its vector span is H₅-stable and V is irreducible. A projective transformation fixing C pointwise is scalar, since C must lie in one of its projectivized eigenspaces and is nondegenerate. Hence N acts faithfully on the smooth normalization X of any N-stable integral C.

For d=deg C≤45, Castelnuovo's bound for a nondegenerate integral curve in P⁴ yields

    g(X)≤π(45,4)=3·14·13/2+14·2=301,

because 45−1=3·14+2. The bound applies to the normalization of a singular integral curve via its birational linear series.

Let Y=X/A. Riemann–Hurwitz gives

    g(Y) ≤ floor((g(X)+24)/25) ≤13.

The induced G=N/A action on Y is faithful: an element of N acting trivially on the invariant function field k(X)^A belongs to the degree-25 Galois deck group A. This contradicts the theorem just proved. Thus no such integral curve of degree at most 45 exists.

## 5. The rank-four curve has degree at least 50 if nonempty

More generally let Γ be a nonzero N-invariant reduced curve without line or conic components and suppose deg Γ≤45. We exhaust the three possible A-orbit sizes of a component C.

If the A-orbit has size 25, its orbit alone has degree at least 25·3=75, impossible.

If it has size five, its A-stabilizer is a one-dimensional axis of F₅². G is transitive on the six axes. Distinct A-orbits having distinct stabilizers are disjoint, so the N-orbit has at least 30 components. Its degree is at least 30·3=90, impossible.

It remains that all components are A-stable. Each has degree divisible by five, as proved by the Heisenberg Hilbert-polynomial argument in ../rank-three-curve-constraints/PROOF.md, Section 2, and hence degree at least five. There are at most nine components. The permutation action on components factors through the perfect group G. A component orbit of size one is excluded by Section 4. Nontrivial orbit sizes below five are impossible because their permutation image would be a perfect subgroup of a solvable symmetric group. Thus any remaining orbit has size m≥5. Its common component degree d is a positive multiple of five, and md≤45 forces d=5.

Section D of ../normalizer-small-curve-exclusion/ADDENDUM_DEGREE_30.md excludes such a small quintic orbit: its normalization is elliptic, A acts by translations, and conjugation on A factors through a cyclic elliptic automorphism quotient of order at most six. Since N/A acts faithfully on A, its component stabilizer satisfies |N_C/A|≤6, so [N:N_C]≥20. Twenty quintics have degree at least 100, a contradiction.

This proves deg Γ>45. Its degree is divisible by five by the same Heisenberg argument, so deg Γ≥50. Apply this to Γ₄ using the imported no-line and no-conic theorems, and combine with deg Γ₄≤125. The result concerns reduced support; it makes no assertion about existence, multiplicity, isolated points, or a scheme-theoretic length.
