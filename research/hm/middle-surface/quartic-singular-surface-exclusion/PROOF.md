# Quartic determinant branches must be singular along a surface

## Result and inputs

Work over C. Let F and B=End₀(F) be as in the preceding structural exclusions. Assume the previously verified inputs c₁(F)=−H, c₂(F)=4H² and H⁰(F(u))=0 for u<3. The existing higher-twist argument proves that a nonzero φ∈H⁰(P⁴,B(2)) has nonzero nonsquare quartic q=−det φ, and its nonempty zero scheme Z is contained scheme-theoretically in the Jacobian singular scheme S of D=V(q).

**Theorem. Every such candidate satisfies dim S≥2.** Thus quartic branches with only curves and isolated points in their singular locus are excluded, including nonreduced singular schemes with those supports. This does not prove H⁰(B(2))=0.

The further bounded conclusions are:

- If q is squarefree, dim S=2, D is nonnormal, but the integral spectral double cover X is normal. The two-dimensional degree of the Jacobian scheme S is at most 9.
- If q is not squarefree, its nonsquare quartic factorization has the form q=L²Q, with L linear and Q a nonsquare quadratic. Then dim S=3 and X is nonnormal.

No HM matrix, actual selected map, source image, or multiplication rank is evaluated.

## 1. Zero scheme and regularity under the excluded hypothesis

The Chern calculation gives

    c₃(B(2)) = (2³+15·2)H³ = 38H³.

A nowhere-vanishing section would leave a locally free rank-two quotient of B(2), forcing this class to vanish. Hence Z is nonempty. Locally, in a frame of F and a trivialization of O(2),

    φ = [[a,b],[c,−a]],    I_Z=(a,b,c),    q=a²+bc.

The generalized principal ideal theorem makes every irreducible component of Z have codimension at most three. The identity q∈I_Z² and its differential give the inclusion Z⊂S as schemes.

Suppose dim S≤1. Every component of Z then has dimension exactly one. At every point of Z, its ideal has height three in the regular local ambient ring and is generated by three elements. In a Cohen–Macaulay local ring, a height-three ideal generated by three elements is generated by a regular sequence. Consequently Z is a local complete intersection of codimension three, is Cohen–Macaulay with no embedded components, and φ is a regular section. The top-Chern formula yields

    [Z]=c₃(B(2))∩[P⁴],    deg Z=38.

Neither Z nor S is assumed reduced. The degree here is the scheme degree, including generic multiplicities.

References for the regular-sequence and zero-section steps: [Stacks, Cohen–Macaulay rings, Lemma 10.104.2](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/00N7) and [Stacks, Chern classes and sections](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/0FA8).

## 2. Three polar cubics produce a containing degree-27 curve

Put R=C[x₀,…,x₄] and J=(q₀,…,q₄), where qᵢ=∂q/∂xᵢ. Euler's identity

    4q = Σ xᵢqᵢ

shows that q is redundant in the homogeneous Jacobian ideal. Thus S=Proj(R/J). Since S contains the nonempty curve Z and has dimension at most one, dim S=1 and ht J=3. Saturation does not affect this height: the affine cone over S has dimension two; the irrelevant vertex cannot increase its dimension.

Let W⊂R₃ be the C-linear span of the partial derivatives. Three sufficiently general elements f₁,f₂,f₃ of W form an R-regular sequence. Here is an explicit existence argument, independent of Bertini smoothness:

- Choose nonzero f₁∈W. R is a domain.
- If f₁,…,fᵣ is regular for r<3, every associated prime of R/(f₁,…,fᵣ) has height r, because this is a complete intersection in a regular ring.
- J cannot be contained in one of those primes, since ht J=3>r. Hence W is not contained in any one of them. Over the infinite field C, a general element of W avoids their finite union and is a non-zero-divisor on the quotient.

Therefore C=V(f₁,f₂,f₃)⊂P⁴ is a complete-intersection curve. The Hilbert series of R/(f₁,f₂,f₃) is

    (1−t³)³/(1−t)⁵ = (1+t+t²)³/(1−t)²,

so deg C=3³=27. Ideal inclusions give

    (f₁,f₂,f₃) ⊂ J ⊂ I_Z

as ideal sheaves, and therefore Z⊂S⊂C scheme-theoretically.

For completeness, degree monotonicity includes all nonreduced structures. The surjection O_C→O_Z has coherent kernel K. Additivity of Hilbert polynomials gives deg C=deg Z+e₁(K), where e₁(K)≥0 is its one-dimensional leading coefficient, taken as zero if its support is zero-dimensional. Equivalently, the surjections at generic curve points compare the corresponding finite lengths. Hence

    38=deg Z ≤ deg C=27,

a contradiction. This proves dim S≥2.

## 3. Squarefree branches: a nonnormal divisor but a normal cover

A squarefree hypersurface over C is generically smooth on each irreducible component. Thus its singular locus has dimension at most two in P⁴. The theorem makes that dimension exactly two.

The three-dimensional hypersurface D is Cohen–Macaulay, hence S₂. At a generic point of a two-dimensional singular component, its one-dimensional local ring is not regular. Thus D fails R₁ and is not normal, whether or not q is irreducible. This uses [Serre's criterion](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/031O).

In contrast, the integral double cover X=Spec_P⁴(O⊕O(−2)), locally z²=q, is a four-dimensional hypersurface, hence S₂. Its singular support is exactly

    z=0, dq=0,

which maps isomorphically as a support to S. The equation q=0 is automatic on this locus. Therefore Sing X has dimension two, of codimension two in X. Its codimension-one points are regular, so X is normal by the same criterion. More generally in this situation, a nonsquare q gives a normal cover exactly when q is squarefree.

One additional degree bound is immediate. For squarefree q, ht J=2, so the preceding prime-avoidance argument selects two regular polar cubics. Their complete-intersection surface has degree 9 and contains S scheme-theoretically. Hilbert-polynomial degree monotonicity gives deg₂ S≤9, where deg₂ denotes the leading two-dimensional scheme degree. This bound allows lower-dimensional or embedded components and counts generic nonreduced multiplicity on the surfaces.

This does not force Z itself to have a surface component. Z might still be a regular curve of degree 38 lying inside a singular surface. Once J has height two, no three elements of J can form a regular sequence: every resulting intersection retains the surface. Thus the degree-27 contradiction cannot be repeated in that residual case.

## 4. Elementary factorization of the remaining quartics

For squarefree reducible q, the degrees of its distinct irreducible factors have one of the partitions

    1+3, 2+2, 1+1+2, 1+1+1+1.

Any two distinct hypersurface factors meet in a surface, and their intersection lies in Sing D. Such reducible quartics already satisfy the dimension obstruction automatically. The theorem therefore does not exclude any entire factorization partition. A squarefree irreducible candidate must be an irreducible nonnormal quartic threefold, singular in codimension one; no classification or nonexistence of such quartics is asserted.

If q is not squarefree, a repeated irreducible factor has degree at most two. A repeated quadratic factor would exhaust degree four and make q a scalar multiple of a square, already excluded over C. Thus some repeated factor is linear L, and

    q=L²Q,   deg Q=2.

Here Q must be nonsquare, otherwise q is square. Accordingly Q is either an irreducible quadratic or the product of two distinct linear forms. The latter includes q=L³M when one factor is L. This is exhaustive up to nonzero scalar. Every partial derivative of L²Q is divisible by L, so the hyperplane L=0 lies in S; hence dim S=3. The singular locus of X likewise has dimension three, causing failure of R₁ and nonnormality. These residual nonnormal-cover cases are not excluded.

## 5. Repeated linear factors do not permit normalization of the module for free

In the residual case q=L²Q, use also the independently established theorem H⁰(B(1))=0. The map φ cannot be divisible by L as a section of B(2), since its quotient would be a nonzero section of B(1). The restriction sequence for the hyperplane H=(L=0) therefore implies that φ|H is nonzero. Since H is integral, it is generically nonzero. Its square is zero and its trace is zero, so its generic rank is one: the candidate retains a nonzero nilpotent action along the repeated branch component.

There is a precise normalization obstruction. The cover

    Y = Spec_P⁴(O⊕O(−1)),    w²=Q,

is integral and normal: a nonsquare quadratic over C is squarefree, so the normality argument above applies. The algebra inclusion defined by z=Lw gives a finite birational map Y→X, hence Y is the normalization of X. Birationality follows over the base function field by w=z/L; finiteness follows because O⊕O(−1) is a finite module even over the smaller base algebra O.

However, the original spectral module cannot acquire an action of this enlarged algebra on the same underlying base bundle F compatible with its existing action. Such an action of w would be a regular map ψ:F→F(1), and compatibility would give

    Lψ=φ,    ψ²=Q·id.

Taking traces in the first equality shows tr ψ=0, because multiplication by nonzero L is injective on sections of a line bundle. Thus ψ∈H⁰(B(1))=0, contradicting φ≠0. In fact divisibility alone already yields the contradiction.

This excludes extending the algebra action on the unchanged F. It does not prohibit pulling M back to the normalization, removing torsion, or taking a reflexive hull there. Those operations can change its finite pushforward, so that pushforward cannot simply be assumed to remain F. Thus the normalization does not reduce these cases to the twist-one exclusion without an additional, presently unjustified module-extension assertion.

## 6. Why this sharpens only the quartic case here

The same method for a degree-2k determinant with dim Sing≤1 would force

    k³+15k ≤ (2k−1)³.

For k=2 this reads 38≤27 and fails. For k=3,4 it reads 72≤125 and 124≤343, producing no contradiction. No new vanishing result at those twists follows.
